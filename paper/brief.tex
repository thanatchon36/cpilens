{\Large\bfseries How much does product classification cost in basis points?}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
{\large Policy brief: classifying Thai product text for price measurement}\par
\vspace{0.4em}
Thanatchon Chongmankhong\quad\textbullet\quad \today\par
\vspace{0.3em}
{\small\itshape The views expressed are the author's own and do not necessarily reflect those
of the Bank of Thailand. This work uses only publicly available data.}
\vspace{0.8em}

\noindent\textbf{Why it matters.} Prices from retailers' websites and scanner data can make
price measurement faster and more detailed. Before such prices can enter a consumer price
index, every product has to be assigned to an item of the official basket: a bag of jasmine
rice to rice, a carton of UHT milk to milk and dairy products. Done by hand this does not
scale; done by a model it introduces errors. This study asks how large those errors would be
in the unit that matters for policy: basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) of
measured inflation.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{What we did.} Using only public data, we tested four kinds of method on
Thai product descriptions: low-cost text-matching models, a fine-tuned language model,
two commercial large language models (LLMs) instructed with a few examples, and
``cascades'' that send only the hard cases to an LLM. Each was scored on \NRefSpecs{}
official Thai price-collection descriptions published by the Ministry of Commerce and held
out from all training, with every item weighted by its share of household spending. We
then fed each method's mistakes through a reconstruction of the Thai CPI to see how far
measured inflation would move.

\subsection*{Three findings}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The best LLM classifies almost all of the basket correctly; low-cost models do
not.} \NameOpus{} assigned \AccWOpus\% of the evaluated CPI weight to the right item and
\NameHaiku{} \AccWHaiku\%. The best low-cost model reached \AccWXlmrFT\%. A cascade that lets a
low-cost model handle the easy cases and sends the rest to \NameOpus{} matched \NameOpus's
accuracy at \CostPctCasEfiveMLPOpus\% of its cost. All LLM runs for this study together cost
USD~\LLMSpendUSD.

\item \textbf{With the best LLM, classification would move headline inflation by about
\BpYoyRmseHeadlineOpus{} basis points.} That is the typical error of the year-on-year rate
over \NMonthsYoy{} months (at most \BpYoyRmseHeadlineOpusHi{} basis points with \CfgCI\%
confidence); for food inflation it is \BpYoyRmseFoodOpus{} basis points. With a low-cost text-matching
model the headline error is \BpYoyRmseHeadlineEfiveMLP{} basis points and the food error
\BpYoyRmseFoodEfiveMLP; another low-cost model would have understated food inflation in every
month.

\item \textbf{Accuracy alone is a poor guide, and better documentation beats a better model.}
Two models with the same accuracy differed several-fold in basis points, because what
matters is whether the confused items' prices moved apart. Most of the LLMs' remaining errors
came from two items whose official contents differ from what their names suggest: the
official ``personal items'' include wristwatches, school bags and hair dryers, and margarine
is counted with milk and dairy products, not with oils and fats.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{What it means for central banks in Asia}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ask for basis points, not accuracy.} The method needs only a test set of labelled
products, the published CPI weights and the published item indices. Any office can use it
to state the classification error of a new data source before the source is used.
\item \textbf{Publish the specification list and the basket's contents.} The Ministry of
Commerce's open specifications made this study possible. A public description of what each
item includes and excludes would remove the largest errors found here, for every method.
\item \textbf{LLMs are affordable at this scale; the issue is governance.} Commercial models
change over time. Responses should be stored and versioned, and an office may prefer a model
it can run itself.
\item \textbf{Languages with few labelled data can start from other languages.} With no Thai
training labels at all, a low-cost model trained on products in other languages placed
\AccWEfiveMLPZeroShot\% of the weight correctly. That is a starting point, not a result an index
can rely on.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Caveats}

The test descriptions are the statistical office's own short texts; shop descriptions are
longer and noisier, and accuracy on them will be lower. The basis-point figures assume a mix
of products in proportion to household spending, cover the \RefWeightEqual\% of the headline
weight that the test set can evaluate, and measure classification error only, not the other
errors of a web-price index. A low basis-point error is meaningful only together with high
accuracy: a model that classifies little of the basket also moves the index little. The
results are for one country and one point in time and are not directly comparable with
studies of other markets.
